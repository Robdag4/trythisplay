Nano blitzes are the most feared — and most misunderstood — part of competitive Madden. This guide covers what a nano actually is in Madden 27, the principles every nano setup is built on, and the adjustments that beat pressure when it's aimed at you.

## What a nano blitz actually is

A nano blitz is not one specific play. It's any pressure setup engineered so a rusher crosses the line of scrimmage untouched, arriving at the quarterback before most routes finish developing. Instead of winning a block, the defense manipulates **who the offensive line chooses to block**, creating a gap nobody accounts for.

Every nano in Madden 27 comes down to the same three ingredients:

1. **An overload** — more threats to one side, or through one gap, than the protection can assign blockers to.
2. **Manipulated blocking logic** — alignment, stems, or shifted linemen that make the offensive line commit to the wrong rushers.
3. **Timing** — adjustments made in an order that keeps the protection from re-sorting before the snap.

That's why nanos get patched and rediscovered every year: the specific plays change, the principles never do.

## Why setup order matters

Two players can call the same defensive play, make the same adjustments, and get completely different results. Pass protection re-evaluates as you make pre-snap changes, so a nano is really a **sequence**: base play, alignment adjustments, individual assignments, and only then the snap. Change the order and the line often re-sorts itself, and your free runner disappears into a double team.

## Building pressure that survives adjustments

A raw nano wins until your opponent slides protection or keeps a back in. Complete pressure schemes in Madden 27 layer three things on top of the free runner:

- **A same-look coverage version.** If your pressure and your coverage look identical pre-snap, opponents can't check out of trouble safely.
- **Answers for extra blockers.** When the back stays in to block, someone is now uncovered — good schemes know in advance who benefits and where the throw will go.
- **Discipline behind the rush.** Nanos give up big plays when the secondary is disorganized. Pressure with cover 0 behind it is a coin flip; pressure with a plan behind it is a scheme.

## Countering nano blitzes

When you're on the wrong side of instant pressure, work through this checklist between snaps:

1. **Find the free runner.** Watch the replay or the rush path — pressure almost always comes from the same gap each time.
2. **Slide protection toward it.** A half-slide changes the line's assignments and forces the blitzer wide.
3. **Add a blocker.** Running back or tight end pass-blocking buys the half-second most nanos can't afford to lose.
4. **Throw hot.** Slants, drags, and quick outs beat pressure that sells out. If they blitz six, someone is open fast.
5. **Punish it.** Screens and draws turn an over-aggressive defense's speed against it. Land one big play and most opponents stop trusting their nano.
